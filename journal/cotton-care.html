Why Your Basics Look Old (And What To Do About It)

September 28, 2026 · 5 min read

A tee that looks tired after four months usually has not worn out. It has been laundered out. The fibers are fine; the color has gone flat, the collar has stretched, and the hem has started to curl — and almost all of that happens in the machine rather than on your body.

None of what follows is specialist advice. It is the handful of habits that separate basics that look good for years from basics that look secondhand by spring.

Heat is the problem

Nearly every way cotton degrades traces back to heat. Hot water opens the fiber and lets dye escape. A hot dryer shrinks the weave, breaks down elastane in ribbed collars and cuffs, and bakes in any stain you did not fully remove.

Two changes fix most of it:

  • Wash cold. Modern detergents are formulated to work at 20–30°C. Hot water is a habit inherited from soap that needed it.
  • Skip the dryer, or use the lowest setting. Air-drying is the single biggest difference you can make. If you do use a dryer, pull things out slightly damp and let them finish on a hanger.
If you change one thing, change the dryer. Heat does more damage to a cotton garment in six months than wearing it does in three years.

The collar problem

A stretched collar is the fastest way a tee starts to look old, and it is almost always mechanical rather than chemical. Two causes:

  • Hanging wet tees. A soaked tee is heavy, and the weight hangs entirely from the collar. Dry them flat, or over a rail at the waist.
  • Pulling them off by the neck. Cross your arms and pull from the hem instead. This sounds fussy and takes one week to become automatic.

Hoodies specifically

Brushed fleece loses its hand when the fibers mat down, which happens from friction and heat together. Turn hoodies inside out before washing — this protects the face of the fabric and the printed or ribbed areas — and never wash them with anything that has a zip or Velcro.

Dry them flat if you have the space. Hanging a wet hoodie stretches the shoulders, and unlike a tee collar, that does not recover.

Trousers and structured pieces

Twill and canvas hold creases, which is usually what you want — but only where you want them. Hang trousers from the waistband or folded over a bar at the knee, not draped over a chair, which sets a crease exactly where it will look accidental.

Structured pieces like an overshirt keep their shape best on a hanger with some shoulder width to it. A wire hanger is worse than no hanger at all.

Wash less

The advice nobody wants: most garments are washed more often than they need to be. A tee worn against the skin for a day, yes. A hoodie worn over a tee for a few hours, almost certainly not. An overshirt worn as a layer, rarely. Airing something overnight handles more than people expect, and every wash you skip is a wash that did not fade it.

The pieces this matters most for are the ones you wear most — the Essential Tee and the Everyday Hoodie, in practice. Those go through the machine several times a month, and they are the ones where a change of habit shows up fastest.
